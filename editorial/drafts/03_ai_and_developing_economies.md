# AI and Developing Economies: From Technological Promise to Public Value

*Why access to artificial intelligence is only the beginning, and why skills, institutions, infrastructure, and governance will determine who benefits.*

**PS04 status:** Draft 1. Editorial policy V2. Source selection approved by the human editor; article text NOT approved for publication.

Artificial intelligence has opened a new chapter in the debate about economic development.

For countries with limited resources, AI offers potentially valuable opportunities: supporting professionals, improving access to information, strengthening public services, and helping institutions perform tasks that previously required substantial technical capacity.

But there is an important distinction between having access to AI and being able to use it effectively. A government can acquire an advanced AI system without possessing the data, infrastructure, skills, or institutional arrangements needed to manage it. An organization can launch an impressive pilot without being able to sustain it. A country can use sophisticated technologies while remaining dependent on capabilities controlled by others.

The central development question is not simply how quickly AI can be adopted. **It is how adoption can translate into lasting productivity, institutional capability, and public value.**

Recent contributions from the World Bank, the International Monetary Fund, FCT&L, and SIGMA help frame this challenge.

## 1. The AI opportunity and the adoption gap

The World Bank's [*Decoding AI: What the Promise of Artificial Intelligence Means for Developing Economies*](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/decoding-ai---what-the-promise-of-artificial-intelligence-means-) argues that benefits depend on complementary capabilities.

Reliable infrastructure, foundational skills, and capable institutions influence whether a country can translate technological opportunities into productivity gains.

Access to sophisticated models may spread, but countries do not begin from equal positions. They differ in digital infrastructure, organizational capacity, skilled labor, and negotiating power. These differences can shape how economic benefits are distributed.

The World Bank analysis also raises the risk of dependence on a small number of providers, particularly when switching suppliers becomes costly. That concern extends beyond technical choices to procurement, public expenditure, data management, and an institution's ability to adapt its systems over time.

AI adoption should therefore be assessed both for immediate functionality and for the capabilities a country develops or retains.

**Technology access creates possibilities. Institutional readiness influences how much of that possibility becomes value.**

## 2. Innovation is more than technological invention

A September 2026 IMF podcast featuring Mehran Gul, [*The Innovation Blindspot*](https://www.imf.org/en/news/podcasts), offers a broader view of innovation.

The discussion explores the geography of innovation, the importance of rules and regulatory environments, and the limitations of using high-growth technology companies as the only evidence of innovative activity.

For developing economies, this perspective opens an important possibility. A country need not create the world's most advanced foundation model to gain value from AI. Innovation may also arise through adapting existing technologies to local needs, redesigning organizational processes, and improving how information or services reach citizens.

A modest application that reliably reduces recurring administrative errors may be more valuable in its setting than a complex pilot with no sustainable operational purpose.

This is an analytical implication of the podcast, not an outcome measured by that discussion. The wider lesson is that innovation policy should recognize adaptation, implementation, and institutional learning alongside invention.

## 3. From pilots to public value

The World Bank's [*From AI Pilots to Scale: Improving Public Services*](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/from-ai-pilots-to-scale--improving-the-delivery-of-public-servic) addresses a challenge faced by many institutions: moving beyond experimentation.

A pilot may establish technical feasibility, but successful demonstration does not ensure sustainable operation across an institution. Deployment requires the capability to procure, test, evaluate, integrate, and maintain systems.

The World Bank discussion highlights **data portability**, **interoperability**, and **auditability**, as well as the institutional capacity needed to sustain operations.

Data portability helps agencies retain access to information when changing suppliers. Interoperability makes it easier for new tools to function alongside existing systems. Auditability helps institutions reconstruct decisions, investigate errors, and improve performance.

**Scaling AI is not simply expanding the number of users or purchasing computing capacity.** It means integrating technology into durable organizational responsibilities, procedures, budgets, and accountability mechanisms.

Without these foundations, a promising pilot may remain a demonstration rather than become a public service.

## 4. Skills are part of development infrastructure

Technological transformation depends on people expected to use new systems.

The September 2026 [FCT&L webinar on essential skills in an AI-enhanced world](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KgI2_dKnpk0) considers how learning objectives and assessments can evolve when AI enters educational and professional environments.

Workers need to learn not only how to operate AI tools, but how to evaluate outputs, apply professional judgment, and recognize the limits of automation.

For public institutions, these skills also involve responsibility to citizens. An employee may know how to obtain an AI-generated recommendation; institutional competence requires knowing when it can be relied upon, when more evidence is needed, and who is accountable for a decision.

The [SIGMA webinar on AI use by civil servants](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1FeLhg9HqfE) addresses the practical use of AI tools in administration and the importance of training among public employees.

Taken together, the discussions suggest that skills development should accompany implementation instead of being added only after problems appear. Training should be evaluated for its effect on work, not just attendance.

**Human capability is not a secondary benefit of digital transformation. It is part of the infrastructure that makes transformation possible.**

## 5. Implications for Cabo Verde

These international perspectives offer a framework for considering AI adoption in Cabo Verde. They do not establish country-specific findings about the nation's current AI readiness. Such an assessment would require separate evidence on public institutions, skills, infrastructure, data systems, and ongoing initiatives.

Consider a hypothetical public-service agency introducing AI to a digital portal. Its initial objective might be helping citizens identify relevant procedures, understand documentation requirements, or navigate administrative services.

Potential benefits should be tested, not assumed. The agency would need to evaluate data quality, accessibility, reliability, and employees' ability to correct mistakes. It would need to examine integration with existing systems and contracts governing the technology.

For public administrations with constrained resources, these choices can influence sustainability. Each AI initiative should be considered both a possible service-improvement project and an opportunity to strengthen internal institutional capacity.

That means preserving knowledge within the organization, developing its workforce, and establishing measurable performance and accountability expectations.

## 6. Five questions for an AI development agenda

A practical policy discussion could begin with five questions:

1. **Which public problems justify AI?** Start with service-delivery needs, not the availability of a particular technology.
2. **What capabilities are missing?** Assess infrastructure, data quality, employee skills, procurement expertise, and supervisory capacity.
3. **How can an institution avoid unnecessary dependence?** Consider interoperability, portability, maintainability, and supplier-transition conditions.
4. **How will employees participate?** Build training, judgment, accountability, and feedback into implementation.
5. **How will public value be measured?** Examine accessibility, reliability, quality, accountability, and sustainable institutional improvement, not just model performance.

These are proposed policy criteria, not a completed country assessment. Their purpose is to shift attention from buying tools toward developing capabilities.

## Conclusion: The development challenge is institutional

Technology alone cannot determine whether AI advances produce broad economic and social benefits. Institutions influence adoption. Skills shape how systems are used. Infrastructure affects reliability. Procurement arrangements influence dependence. Accountability determines whether AI-enabled decisions can be trusted and corrected.

The World Bank's analyses emphasize complementary capabilities and sustainable implementation. The IMF podcast broadens the discussion of innovation. FCT&L and SIGMA highlight human capabilities in AI-enhanced environments.

Together, these sources support a central proposition: **the strongest development strategy is not simply to acquire AI capabilities, but to build the institutional capacity to turn them into lasting public value.**

For Cabo Verde and other developing economies, that is an agenda for careful investigation, experimentation, and institutional learning.
