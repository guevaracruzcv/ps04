# AI at Work: Why the Real Question Is Bigger Than Job Loss

*The future of employment will depend not only on what artificial intelligence can automate, but also on how organizations redesign work, develop skills, and involve people in decisions.*

**PS04 status:** Draft 1. Editorial policy V2. Source selection approved by the human editor; article text NOT approved for publication.

Artificial intelligence has revived a familiar debate: will new technology create more jobs than it destroys?

It is an important question, but perhaps not the most useful place to begin. Focusing exclusively on the number of jobs that might disappear risks overlooking changes already taking place inside organizations: how tasks are allocated, how workers acquire new skills, who benefits from productivity gains, and who participates in decisions about technological adoption.

Four recent sources, from Harvard, MIT Sloan, Reuters, and an educational webinar on AI-era skills, suggest a more productive way to examine the future of work.

## 1. The difference between automating tasks and eliminating jobs

In September 2026, the [Harvard Gazette examined why artificial intelligence has not yet triggered widespread mass layoffs](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2026/09/why-ai-hasnt-triggered-mass-layoffs-yet/), despite growing concern about technological displacement.

The article does not suggest that employment risks have disappeared. It acknowledges reports of layoffs attributed to AI and examines why employers may nevertheless have reasons to retain human workers.

A technology capable of performing a particular task does not automatically replace the entire occupation in which that task appears. Jobs usually involve combinations of responsibilities: technical execution, judgment, communication, coordination, relationships, and accountability.

Automation may therefore transform a job before eliminating it. It may remove some responsibilities, create others, or change what employers expect from their workforce.

That does not mean the transition will be painless. Workers can face disrupted career paths, reduced bargaining power, or new qualification requirements even when their occupations survive.

**The central question is not simply how many jobs AI eliminates. It is how the technology changes the quality, security, and value of work.**

## 2. Workers should participate in AI adoption

An important contribution comes from [MIT Sloan's analysis of Kaiser Permanente's AI labor agreement](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/what-employers-can-learn-kaiser-permanentes-ai-labor-deal).

The article examines negotiations between Kaiser Permanente and the Alliance of Health Care Unions, which represents healthcare workers. It highlights the importance of trusted worker representatives in communicating about AI adoption.

Organizations often approach technological transformation primarily as a management and engineering challenge. They assess technology, estimate productivity benefits, select software, and plan implementation.

Employees experience these changes through different questions. Will their responsibilities change? Will their expertise remain valuable? Will they receive training? How will mistakes be handled? Who will decide which tasks become automated?

Worker involvement creates opportunities to identify operational risks that may be invisible at the executive level. It can also make adoption more transparent and improve organizational decisions.

The Kaiser Permanente case is not proof that every labor agreement produces successful AI implementation. It illustrates why **participation and institutional trust belong inside the design of technological change**, rather than simply in communications after decisions have been made.

## 3. Productivity gains do not automatically become wage gains

The [Reuters podcast *AI at Work*](https://pdst.fm/e/traffic.megaphone.fm/THRH6042123585.mp3), published in October 2026, adds another dimension: employment mobility and wages.

The discussion examines why technological change cannot be understood solely through job-loss predictions. It raises the importance of opportunities for workers to move between jobs and improve their earnings.

An organization may become more productive without every employee experiencing a proportional improvement in wages or working conditions.

Whether gains reach workers depends on more than the performance of technology. Labor-market opportunities, bargaining arrangements, skills, and management decisions can influence how those gains are distributed.

A useful public-policy question emerges: **how can societies encourage innovation while ensuring that workers have credible opportunities to adapt and benefit?**

Possible responses include accessible retraining, support for career transitions, and careful monitoring of employment and wage outcomes. These are policy options to investigate, not conclusions automatically established by the podcast.

## 4. Education must prepare people for changing tasks

A September 2026 [FCT&L discussion on essential skills in an AI-enhanced world](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KgI2_dKnpk0) addresses the education and skills dimension.

The webinar considers how educational institutions should define capabilities students need when AI becomes part of their learning and professional environments. It also describes work combining human expertise and AI in identifying learning objectives and mastery skills.

Preparing people for AI should not mean teaching everyone to use the same software tool. Tools will evolve, and responsibilities will change with them.

A more durable approach develops the capacity to evaluate information, exercise judgment, collaborate, learn continuously, and recognize when automated outputs require verification.

Technical AI literacy matters, but so does understanding the limits of a system. The educational challenge is not simply to train people to operate AI; it is to prepare them to work effectively in environments where human and automated capabilities interact.

## 5. The challenge for public institutions

These lessons are particularly relevant to public administration. Governments are employers, regulators, educators, and providers of essential services. They must consider both how AI affects their employees and how technological change influences the wider labor market.

For Cabo Verde, the policy implications deserve careful consideration. Questions concern not only access to AI tools, but also institutional readiness, professional development, accountability, and the ability to convert innovation into public value. This is a proposed application of the analysis, not a finding made by the four sources about Cabo Verde.

Three priorities merit examination. First, AI adoption should begin with an understanding of tasks and institutional objectives, rather than assuming automation itself represents progress. Second, workforce development should accompany implementation. Third, public institutions should measure results beyond efficiency, including service quality, fairness, employee capabilities, and accountability.

## Conclusion: The future of work is also a governance question

AI will continue to change how organizations perform tasks. Some occupations may be transformed, others may contract, and new responsibilities may emerge. The eventual distribution of benefits depends on technological capabilities and on decisions about skills, employee participation, economic adjustment, and institutional oversight.

The most useful debate is therefore not simply **human versus machine**. It is about how societies organize the relationship between people and increasingly capable technologies.
