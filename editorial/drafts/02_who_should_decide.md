# Who Should Decide? AI Autonomy, Human Oversight, and Institutional Accountability

*Artificial intelligence can help governments make decisions faster. But technical capability is not the same as institutional authority, and successful adoption depends on knowing the difference.*

**PS04 status:** Draft 1. Editorial policy V2. Source selection approved by the human editor; article text NOT approved for publication.

Artificial intelligence is creating opportunities for public institutions to improve how they work. Governments can use AI to organize information, support administrative processes, identify patterns, and assist professionals facing complex decisions.

Yet a fundamental question remains: **when should AI be allowed to make a decision, and when should it support a human decision-maker instead?**

The answer cannot depend only on whether a system is technically capable of producing a result. Public decisions affect people, rights, resources, and access to services. They operate within laws, institutional responsibilities, and expectations of fairness and accountability.

Recent contributions from MIT Sloan, SIGMA, and the World Bank suggest that the challenge is not simply deploying more advanced technology. It is establishing the institutional capacity to decide where, how, and under whose authority that technology should operate.

## 1. Technical capability does not automatically justify autonomy

A September 2026 [MIT Sloan article on an AI Decision Matrix](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/a-framework-determining-when-ai-can-make-decisions) offers a useful starting point.

The framework considers two dimensions: **the ambiguity of a decision and the risks associated with getting it wrong**. Routine decisions involving relatively little ambiguity and low risk can be candidates for automation. Decisions with high uncertainty and substantial consequences require stronger human leadership.

Consider two hypothetical uses of AI within a public-service agency. One system checks whether an application contains all the required documents. Another recommends whether a citizen should qualify for a consequential public benefit.

Both may involve similar technical components, but they do not carry the same responsibility. The first task can potentially be automated with validation and correction procedures. The second may require legal interpretation, contextual judgment, procedural fairness, and mechanisms for challenging errors.

**The right question is not merely whether AI can perform a task. It is whether the institution can justify delegating authority over that task.**

## 2. Human oversight must have operational meaning

Organizations frequently describe AI systems as operating under human supervision. But the presence of a person somewhere in a workflow does not, by itself, establish effective oversight.

An institution should explain what employees review, which decisions they can override, and how errors are detected and corrected.

For low-risk administrative tasks, oversight may consist of routine quality checks and exception handling. For consequential decisions, it may require examination of evidence, explanations of recommendations, and meaningful authority to reject a proposed outcome.

If an employee can theoretically override an AI recommendation but lacks the information, training, or time needed to evaluate it, the arrangement may be ineffective. Likewise, manager approval does not necessarily establish accountability when the institution cannot reconstruct why a result was produced.

Human oversight should therefore be supported by clear responsibilities, professional judgment, documentation, and review procedures. These are governance principles derived from the wider accountability problem, rather than findings independently demonstrated by a single source.

## 3. Civil servants are central to responsible adoption

The September 2026 [SIGMA webinar on the use of AI by civil servants](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1FeLhg9HqfE) brings this discussion into daily public administration.

The webinar addresses the introduction of AI in administrative environments, the use of available tools, and the importance of developing capabilities among public employees.

Employees need to understand what AI technology does, what it cannot reliably do, and when outputs require further scrutiny. They need practical guidance on handling information, recognizing uncertainty, and escalating decisions outside the intended scope of an automated system.

Training should connect technological skills with the legal, operational, and ethical obligations of public service. The role of the civil servant is not necessarily diminished by AI; in some workflows it may shift toward verification, interpretation, exception handling, and responsibility for outcomes.

A government that introduces AI tools without investing in its workforce risks creating a gap between technical sophistication and the institutional capacity to control it.

## 4. Moving from pilots to sustainable public services

The World Bank's [*From AI Pilots to Scale: Improving Public Services*](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/from-ai-pilots-to-scale--improving-the-delivery-of-public-servic) offers a broader institutional perspective.

The article emphasizes that successful adoption requires the capacity to procure, test, evaluate, integrate, and maintain AI solutions. A pilot may demonstrate that a technology works under controlled conditions without establishing that it can operate reliably across an institution, integrate with existing infrastructure, or remain useful after the initial project team leaves.

The World Bank discussion identifies **data portability**, **interoperability**, **auditability**, and sustained institutional capacity as important requirements.

Data portability can reduce reliance on a single supplier. Interoperability helps applications work alongside existing government information systems. Auditability supports review of decisions and correction of errors. Sustained technical capacity allows an institution to manage systems throughout their operational life.

Governments should consequently evaluate projects not only by initial performance but by whether they can govern those systems over time. Moving from experimentation to operational deployment is an institutional transition, not merely a technology deployment.

## 5. Developing economies face particular constraints

A second World Bank article, [*Decoding AI: What the Promise of Artificial Intelligence Means for Developing Economies*](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/decoding-ai---what-the-promise-of-artificial-intelligence-means-), argues that AI benefits depend on complementary conditions, including reliable infrastructure, foundational skills, and capable institutions.

It also highlights risks when developing economies rely on technologies controlled by a small number of providers, especially when changing suppliers becomes costly.

Access to advanced models does not automatically produce development benefits. Institutions differ in infrastructure, expertise, resources, and bargaining power, affecting what they can implement and sustain.

For resource-constrained governments, a central question is whether AI investments build durable public capabilities or introduce dependencies that become difficult to manage. The answer calls for strategic institutional choices rather than assuming that adoption is either inherently beneficial or inherently harmful.

## 6. A practical public-sector decision checklist

Taken together, the sources suggest five questions that public institutions can use when examining a proposed AI application:

1. **What decision is being supported or automated?** Distinguish routine processing from recommendations and consequential public decisions.
2. **How ambiguous and risky is the decision?** Calibrate human authority to the possible consequences of error.
3. **Who is accountable?** Assign responsibility for supervision, correction, escalation, and review.
4. **Can the institution operate and audit the system?** Assess data quality, staff skills, procurement conditions, interoperability, and independent examination.
5. **How will success be measured over time?** Look at service quality, fairness, reliability, staff capacity, and actual public value, not just processing speed.

This is a proposed checklist synthesized from the sources, not a substitute for applicable legal requirements.

## 7. Implications for Cabo Verde

For Cabo Verde, these questions offer a framework for examining future digital public services. They do not establish factual findings about specific AI deployments in Cabo Verde; that would require country-specific evidence.

Imagine a hypothetical portal using AI to help citizens identify procedures, understand documentation, or navigate administrative services. Such uses may provide benefits that should be tested. But if the system begins making eligibility recommendations or influencing legal obligations, stronger governance arrangements become necessary.

The institution would need to evaluate data quality, employee authority, process transparency, and citizens' ability to seek explanation and correction. It would also need to examine sustainability, integration with existing government systems, and contractual terms.

The objective should be to use AI to strengthen administrative capability while retaining public accountability.

## Conclusion: Govern decisions, not only technology

AI creates opportunities to improve public services and support civil servants. It does not remove the responsibility to decide how public authority is exercised.

The MIT Sloan framework distinguishes decisions by ambiguity and risk. The SIGMA discussion puts civil servants and their skills at the center of adoption. The World Bank emphasizes the institutional foundations needed to move beyond pilots.

Together, they point toward a common principle: **AI autonomy should expand only as institutional capacity, oversight, and accountability can support it.**
