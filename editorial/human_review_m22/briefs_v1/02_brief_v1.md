Title: Public-sector AI claims need context, controls and human accountability

Predecessor: editorial/human_review_m19/briefs_v1/consolidated_briefs_v1.jsonl

Research question: Which claims about AI in consequential public services withstand scrutiny, and what evidence is still missing?

Argument: AI research and public-sector plans can demonstrate technical promise, but neither a successful experiment nor a persuasive output establishes that a system is safe, effective or suitable for a government decision. Scrutiny should distinguish technical results from deployment evidence, examine the consequences of errors, and identify who remains accountable.

Synthesis: MIT reports that its planned Public Transit Intelligence Hub aims to bring fragmented operational information together for transit staff and is intended to leave operational decisions to those staff. Separately, MIT researchers report that HardFlow satisfied constraints in experiments involving robotics, control and computer vision; those experimental results are not evidence that the method has been deployed or validated in government. The MIT Sloan decision matrix offers a framework based on ambiguity and risk, but its reported interviews and company example are enterprise evidence, not public-administration findings. In a Sigma webinar, presenters identified data protection and accuracy as concerns raised by participants. That poll indicates concerns among responding webinar participants, not a representative survey of the wider civil service. Across these examples, the central test is not simply whether an AI system can produce an output, but whether its role, limits, evidence and human responsibility are clear in the context where it would be used. Harvard Gazette adds an organizational capability and evaluation perspective: its account describes retraining for AI-augmented roles followed by testing whether that training works. This complements MIT technical and decision-support examples and SIGMA participant concerns; it does not establish successful public-sector outcomes.

Limitations: The transit article describes project aims and planned development, not measured service outcomes. HardFlow’s reported results are experimental and concern specific technical tasks. The MIT Sloan framework is based on executive interviews and an enterprise case. Sigma webinar poll results reflect attendees and should not be generalized to civil servants as a whole. None of these sources, alone, establishes that any particular AI system is safe for a specific public decision. The Harvard account describes a research and organizational training approach involving firms, not proven training effectiveness or evidence of public-sector results.
